01  Overview

A redesign of the tool
that builds the tools.

PatternBuilder is the NetDocuments app builder. Legal teams use it to turn how they work into apps: an intake form that opens a matter, an engagement letter that fills itself in, a workflow that saves the result to the right place in the document management system. NetDocuments launched it in 2022, built on its acquisition of Afterpattern.

The builder worked, and firms had built real apps in it. But it had grown feature by feature, and the people who build those apps, usually admins and power users rather than developers, paid for it in hunting, guessing and second checks. I led the redesign of the builder: the strategy, a full audit of what existed, the new structure, and much of the front end itself, which I built in the production codebase alongside engineering.

02  The problem

Powerful, but it
made you hunt.

The old builder put everything in one long scroll under a row of tabs: Blocks, Templates, Settings and Snapshots, with Everything, Pages and Logic filters below them and a Show variables toggle off to the side. It looked like none of the rest of NetDocuments, which uses a sidebar.

The bigger costs were invisible. Variables, the names that carry an answer from one page into a document, lived inside the blocks that created them, so an app with 30 or more variables meant scrolling to find where something was defined. How blocks related to each other was not visible on the canvas. There was no sign of which block you were working in, deletes used the browser's own confirm box, and the button that took an app live sat at the same weight as everything around it, with no signal that the app was ready.

03  My role and how I worked

Strategy first,
then screens, then code.

A redesign of a tool people have already built on is a product problem before it is a design problem. So I started with the plan, not the pixels. I wrote the modernization strategy and a phased game plan, reviewed them with product and engineering, and ran a full audit of the builder so every later decision could point at a real gap.

The strategy set the rules: reduce cognitive load, make intent clear, move to Fluent, the design system the rest of the platform is adopting, and avoid disruptive changes. It also set the non-goals, which mattered as much: no ground up rebuild, no large changes in behavior without validation. Then I prototyped the new builder and built much of it myself, using AI coding tools in the real codebase so engineering reviewed working UI instead of specs. Five decisions shaped it.

Decision 01

Modernize in place, not rebuild

Firms had live apps in PatternBuilder. A rebuild would have frozen new work for months and risked breaking them. I chose to keep the engine and the block model and change the experience around it, shipped in phases ranked by risk and turned on one customer at a time.

Decision 02

A left rail, like the rest of NetDocuments

The row of tabs became a fixed left rail for Blocks, Templates, Snapshots and Settings, with help and feedback at the bottom. The builder finally matches the platform it lives in, and the canvas gets the full width.

Decision 03

A panel that shows the whole app

A new right panel lists every block in order with its type and what it holds, such as "Question page, 2 questions, 1 action". A Variables tab lists every variable with how many times it is used, so builders stop scrolling to find where something lives.

Decision 04

Choosing, not remembering

Add block, Add content and Add action became dialogs with search and tabs: pages, logic and navigation for blocks; questions, elements and documents for content. Every option is named and visible, so builders choose from what exists instead of remembering it.

Decision 05

One primary action, and guardrails around it

Publish is the one blue button, with Preview beside it and an Editing status. Deleting a block now explains what goes with it. Preview stops with a plain message that names the block causing the problem, instead of failing later.

04  The audit

107 features,
68 pain points.

Before redesigning anything I listed everything the builder could do, area by area, and noted what was wrong with each part. That audit became the shared map for product and engineering, and the source of the top gaps the redesign set out to close.

Navigation that did not match

Horizontal tabs, while the rest of NetDocuments uses a sidebar. Builders had to learn a second way to move around.

Variables buried in blocks

Variables lived inside the blocks that created them, which hurts most in large apps with 30 or more of them.

Invisible structure

How blocks connect, and which one you are working in, did not show on the canvas.

No readiness signal

Going live sat next to testing at the same weight, with nothing to say whether an app was ready.

06  Starting and staying safe

Guardrails where
mistakes cost the most.

Most builder mistakes are found late: an app published to the wrong place, a block deleted with everything inside it, a preview that fails without saying why. I put the decision or the warning at the moment it matters.

07  Rollout

Shipping a redesign
people already rely on.

Because firms depend on their apps every day, how the redesign reaches them was part of the design. I ranked every change by risk into three rollout phases, lowest risk first, and the new builder is turned on for one customer at a time behind a feature flag. That lets the team learn from real builders before anyone is moved by default.

Success is defined by what builders do, not how the screens look: task success, error rates and support feedback, with in-product feedback built in from day one. The new builder is rolling out now, so I will add results here once they are measured. Next in the plan are deeper tools for large apps, like data tables in the right panel.

08  Impact

What changed for builders.

One platform, one pattern

The builder now uses the same navigation and Fluent components as the rest of NetDocuments, so it no longer feels like a separate product.

The whole app at a glance

Builders can see every block and every variable without scrolling the canvas, the gaps the audit ranked highest.

Fewer late surprises

Where an app lives is set at creation, deletes explain themselves, and preview points to the block that needs work.

A designer who ships

Much of the new front end is code I wrote and engineering reviewed, which shortened the path from design decision to working software.
